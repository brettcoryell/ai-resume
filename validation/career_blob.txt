Purdue biographical session — Customer-Driven Project Governance at Purdue.

By the end of the first year, Jeff and I both knew we were going to have to prepare the organization to put projects on hold or slow them down. There was no current mechanism for doing that — only long-standing one-off relationships and side deals negotiated with the prior CIO. We both wanted to put in a real project governance system. The most interesting thing about how we set it up was this: IT doesn't get a vote. We are the governed. I would provide information, cost things out, estimate delays, talk about cross-cutting impacts we could see, and even give a personal perspective if asked — but all our opinions were to be taken as just opinions. Ultimately, we were happy to do what the assembled wisdom of the group decided was best for the university.

That meant putting the customers in charge. The senior stakeholders decided which projects were accepted, accelerated, slowed down, or canceled. They had to have difficult conversations with each other about projects that were struggling — about scope and quality and budget — and then vote project against project. Which ones would get the attention and the limited resources, and which ones would be paused or canceled early.

Getting that group to look each other in the eye, have the hard conversations, and accept the difficult realities was a real accomplishment. Purdue's senior stakeholders did it better than any other organization I worked with. Part of it was the people — the Comptroller, the head of the student area — but part of it was the moment. Everybody knew the ERP was coming. They'd watched their colleagues at peer institutions go through it. They knew it was going to be hard, they knew they'd have to give up their staff, and they knew they couldn't do everything. When we were finally able to show all the projects already in flight in a single place, there was a quiet moment in the room as people looked at it and said: we have all of this, and we have that enormous thing coming. Purdue biographical session — Critical Chain at Purdue.

At Sprint I had become a fan of a book called Critical Chain, which introduces the concept of schedule buffers. The core insight is that people naturally let work expand to fill the time allotted, and delay starting until the last possible moment. If someone thinks it will take three days to patch a certain set of systems, they'll tell you it will take five. Give them ten days and they'll start two or three days before it's due. Every step of the chain accumulates fake slack, and the project manager and director have to go hunting through the schedule trying to figure out where it's all hiding — usually over resistance and excuses about why something can't possibly be done any faster.

Pulling that hidden slack out into shared, visible project buffers — or buffers for sections of a project — is genuinely useful. Jeff had seen the concept before, and as my project managers became more skilled, we started putting it into practice at Purdue.

Schedule buffers and customer-driven project governance were the two areas where I was able to bring something to Jeff that he didn't have much practical experience with. Both were nice additions to the portfolio of work we did together at Purdue.
